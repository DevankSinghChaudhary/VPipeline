# Sentence Rhythm

Purpose

Structure sentences so they sound natural, clear, and engaging when spoken by Text-to-Speech.

---

## Core Principle

Write for the ear, not the eye.

If text would look great in a document but sounds weird out loud, rewrite it.

The listener cannot reread a sentence. Sentence structure must therefore support natural speech, clear comprehension, and a comfortable rhythm.

---

## Natural Spoken Rhythm

A natural narration should not sound like every sentence has the same structure or length.

Vary:

* Sentence length.
* Sentence openings.
* The number of clauses.
* The placement of pauses.
* The rhythm between short and long sentences.

Example:

The economy was already weakening.

Shortages were becoming more common, and public frustration was growing.

Then, the government introduced a new series of reforms.

---

## Avoid Monotonous Sentence Patterns

Do not make every sentence follow the same structure.

Bad:

The government introduced reforms. The government changed the economy. The government faced opposition.

Better:

The government introduced a series of reforms. They changed the economy, but opposition quickly grew.

---

## Short Sentences

Use short sentences when:

* A fact needs emphasis.
* The previous sentence contains several ideas.
* The listener needs time to process information.
* A scene or idea has changed.

Example:

The system was already failing.

Then came the crisis.

---

## Long Sentences

Longer sentences are acceptable when the ideas are closely connected and the sentence remains easy to follow when spoken.

Example:

As the economy weakened and shortages became more common, public frustration grew across the country.

Do not make a sentence long merely to combine information.

---

## Sentence Length Variation

Avoid sequences of sentences with identical lengths.

Bad:

The economy weakened. The government responded. The public became angry. The protests began.

Better:

The economy weakened. The government responded with a series of reforms, but public frustration continued to grow. Soon, the protests began.

---

## One-Listen Comprehension

The listener should be able to understand the sentence without rereading it.

If a sentence requires the listener to remember too many clauses or details before reaching its main point, rewrite it.

Bad:

The reforms, which were introduced after years of economic decline and political pressure that had already caused widespread dissatisfaction among the population, eventually created new problems.

Better:

The reforms came after years of economic decline and political pressure. But instead of solving the crisis, they created new problems.

---

## Clause Density

Avoid packing too many independent ideas into one sentence.

Bad:

The economy weakened, the government introduced reforms, public frustration grew, and protests eventually spread across the country.

Better:

The economy weakened, and the government responded with reforms. Public frustration continued to grow. Eventually, protests spread across the country.

---

## Sentence Openings

Vary sentence openings naturally.

Avoid repeatedly beginning sentences with the same structure.

Bad:

The government introduced reforms.

The government faced opposition.

The government changed its policy.

Better:

The government introduced reforms. Opposition quickly followed. Under growing pressure, its policy began to change.

---

## Rhythm Around Important Facts

Place important facts where they are easy to hear and understand.

A major fact may deserve its own sentence.

Example:

The reforms failed.

That failure would shape the country's politics for years to come.

---

## Factual Continuity

Do not split sentences in a way that separates facts that must be understood together.

Bad:

The government introduced reforms.

These reforms eventually accelerated the collapse.

Better:

The government introduced reforms that eventually accelerated the collapse.

---

## Sentence Fusion

Keep closely related facts together when combining them creates more natural speech.

Example:

The economy weakened, and shortages became more common.

Do not split every clause into a separate sentence.

---

## Sentence Splitting

Split a sentence when:

* It contains too many independent ideas.
* The main point is difficult to identify.
* The listener must hold too much information in memory.
* The sentence sounds unnatural when spoken.
* A new sentence would create a natural rhythm.

---

## What to Avoid

These patterns break the illusion of natural speech in a TTS engine.

### Parentheses

Avoid parentheses.

TTS often reads them awkwardly. Rewrite the information as a separate clause or sentence.

### Lists and Markdown

Avoid bullet points, numbered lists, and markdown formatting in spoken narration.

A voice agent is speaking, not writing. Structure responses through natural sentence flow.

### Excessive Exclamation Marks

Do not use back-to-back exclamation marks.

Use no more than one exclamation mark per response unless the source material specifically requires otherwise.

Overuse can sound unnatural or overly excited.

### ALL CAPS for Emphasis

Do not use ALL CAPS for emphasis.

Use word choice, sentence structure, and natural phrasing to convey emphasis.

Reserve capitalization for abbreviations, acronyms, proper nouns, and other cases where it is grammatically or semantically required.

### Repetitive Sentence Openings

Avoid consecutive sentences that start with the same word or phrase.

Repeated sentence openings can sound repetitive and mechanical when spoken aloud.

Bad:

The government introduced reforms.

The government faced opposition.

The government changed its policy.

Better:

The government introduced reforms.

Opposition quickly followed.

Under growing pressure, its policy began to change.

---

## Spoken Transitions

Use natural transitions when they improve spoken flow.

Examples:

But

However

Meanwhile

Then

Eventually

As a result

At the same time

For a moment

These transitions should clarify the relationship between ideas, not be added mechanically.

---

## Rhythm and Emphasis

Sentence structure can naturally emphasize important information.

Compare:

The reforms eventually caused the system to collapse.

The reforms failed.

Eventually, the entire system collapsed.

Use shorter structures when a fact deserves greater spoken emphasis.

Do not create artificial drama.

---

## Paragraph Rhythm

A paragraph should not sound like one continuous block of information.

Use sentence boundaries to create a natural progression:

1. Introduce the situation.
2. Develop the idea.
3. Add relevant detail.
4. Deliver the result or consequence.

---

## Written Style vs Spoken Style

Written:

The discovery, which occurred during a period of rapid technological development, fundamentally changed the way researchers understood the problem.

Spoken:

The discovery happened during a period of rapid technological development. It fundamentally changed how researchers understood the problem.

Prefer the version that is easier to understand when heard.

---

## Read-Aloud Test

Before finalizing a sentence, evaluate it as spoken audio.

Ask:

* Would a human naturally say it this way?
* Can the listener understand it on the first listen?
* Does the sentence have a natural rhythm?
* Is the sentence carrying too many ideas?
* Does the next sentence follow naturally?
* Does it sound like speech rather than a written document?

If the sentence looks good but sounds unnatural, rewrite it.

---

## Rule Priority

1. Preserve meaning.
2. Preserve factual accuracy.
3. Preserve natural spoken rhythm.
4. Ensure one-listen comprehension.
5. Vary sentence structure and length.
6. Create natural transitions.
7. Preserve grammatical correctness.
8. Optimize visual appearance last.

---

## Never

* Never write only for visual appearance.
* Never make every sentence the same length.
* Never use long sentences simply to fit more information.
* Never split every sentence into short fragments.
* Never force dramatic rhythm.
* Never sacrifice factual continuity for shorter sentences.
* Never add transitions mechanically.
* Never preserve a sentence that looks correct but sounds unnatural when spoken.
* Never forget: write for the ear, not the eye.
