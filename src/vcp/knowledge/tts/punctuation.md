# Punctuation

Purpose

Format punctuation for natural spoken delivery. The goal is not to make text look grammatically or visually perfect on a page. The goal is to make it sound natural when heard.

---

## Core Principle

Write for the ear, not the eye.

If text would look great in a document but sounds weird out loud, rewrite it.

Every punctuation decision should be evaluated by how the sentence sounds when spoken, not only by how it appears visually.

---

## Spoken Naturalness

Prioritize:

- Natural breathing points.
- Natural pauses.
- Clear sentence rhythm.
- Easy comprehension when heard once.
- Smooth transitions between ideas.

Do not prioritize:

- Maximum grammatical complexity.
- Visually dense sentence structures.
- Formal punctuation patterns that sound unnatural when spoken.
- Punctuation added only because a written document would normally contain it.

---

## Commas

Use commas to create short, natural pauses.

Example:

After the war, the country entered a period of instability.

However, the situation continued to deteriorate.

The economy weakened, and shortages became more common.

---

## Comma Overuse

Do not insert commas wherever a speaker might theoretically pause.

Too many commas create unnatural stop-and-start speech.

Bad:

The government, after years of decline, introduced, a series of reforms, that changed the economy.

Better:

After years of decline, the government introduced a series of reforms that changed the economy.

---

## Long Sentences

If a sentence contains too many ideas, do not try to solve the problem with additional commas.

Rewrite or split the sentence.

Bad:

The economy had already begun to weaken, shortages were becoming more common, the government continued to prioritize military production, and public frustration was growing.

Better:

The economy had already begun to weaken. Shortages were becoming more common, while the government continued to prioritize military production. Public frustration was growing.

---

## Sentence Boundaries

Use periods when the listener needs a clear separation between ideas.

A period is often better than a comma when:

- The thought is complete.
- The next sentence introduces a new fact.
- The sentence is becoming difficult to follow when spoken.
- The listener needs time to process the previous idea.

---

## Semicolons

Use semicolons sparingly.

Although semicolons are grammatically correct, they can sound formal or unnatural in spoken narration.

Prefer a period or a conjunction when it produces more natural speech.

Written:

The economy was weakening; the reforms accelerated the crisis.

Natural narration:

The economy was weakening. The reforms accelerated the crisis.

---

## Colons

Use colons before explanations, lists, or conclusions when the pause sounds natural.

Example:

The situation had three major causes: economic decline, political instability, and nationalist movements.

When a colon creates an unnatural formal pause, rewrite the sentence.

---

## Dashes

Use em dashes to represent a genuine interruption, contrast, or noticeable change in thought.

Example:

The reforms were intended to save the system — but they accelerated its collapse.

Use dashes sparingly.

Do not use dashes merely to make narration sound dramatic.

---

## Parentheses

Parentheses are usually written for the eye and may sound unnatural when spoken.

Rewrite parenthetical information into the sentence whenever possible.

Written:

The reforms (introduced in 1985) changed the system.

Natural:

The reforms, introduced in 1985, changed the system.

Do not remove important information contained inside parentheses.

---

## Ellipses

Use ellipses only when they represent a genuine unfinished thought, hesitation, or intentionally extended pause.

Example:

For a moment, nobody moved...

Do not use ellipses as a general dramatic effect.

---

## Quotation Marks

Preserve quotation marks when they represent spoken words, titles, or quoted material.

Example:

He described the situation as "a turning point."

If a quotation is long or difficult to follow when heard, use natural sentence structure and attribution to make the spoken meaning clear.

---

## Apostrophes and Contractions

Preserve natural contractions.

Examples:

don't

it's

can't

wouldn't

Contractions usually sound more natural in narration than their expanded forms.

Do not expand contractions unless a specific pronunciation rule requires it.

---

## Punctuation and Breathing

Punctuation should create natural breathing and processing points.

A pause should occur where a human narrator would naturally pause.

Do not force a pause simply because a comma is grammatically possible.

---

## Written Structure vs Spoken Structure

Written structure:

The discovery, which occurred during a period of rapid technological development, fundamentally changed the way researchers understood the problem.

Natural spoken structure:

The discovery happened during a period of rapid technological development. It fundamentally changed how researchers understood the problem.

Use the spoken structure when the written structure is difficult to follow aloud.

---

## One-Listen Comprehension

The listener may not be able to reread the sentence.

If a sentence is difficult to understand on the first listen, rewrite it.

Prefer:

- Shorter sentences.
- Clear relationships between ideas.
- Natural pauses.
- Direct sentence structures.

Avoid:

- Long chains of subordinate clauses.
- Multiple parenthetical interruptions.
- Excessive commas.
- Dense written-style sentence structures.

---

## Punctuation Priority

When deciding how to punctuate or restructure text, prioritize:

1. Meaning.
2. Factual accuracy.
3. Natural spoken rhythm.
4. One-listen comprehension.
5. Natural breathing points.
6. Grammatical correctness.
7. Visual appearance on the page.

---

## Rule Priority

Write for the ear, not the eye.

If a sentence looks correct but sounds unnatural when spoken, rewrite the sentence.

If punctuation improves visual appearance but harms spoken rhythm, prioritize spoken rhythm.

If a sentence is grammatically correct but difficult to understand when heard once, simplify or restructure it.

---

## Never

- Never add punctuation solely to make text look better on a page.
- Never overuse commas to represent every possible pause.
- Never use semicolons when a period sounds more natural.
- Never use dashes for artificial drama.
- Never use ellipses as a general pause marker.
- Never preserve a complex written sentence if it sounds unnatural when spoken.
- Never sacrifice natural speech rhythm for visual formatting.
- Never change the meaning while rewriting for speech.
